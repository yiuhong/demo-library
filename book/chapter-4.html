Chapter 4 – Association

Yiu Hong Ko · Volume 1 · aligned with demo-library · as of 26 Nov 2026 HKT

Chapter learning goals

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Explain covariance as shared movement in the units of both variables, and correlation as that movement on a scale from −1 to 1.
  • Explain how ordinary least squares chooses the line, and the plane on two predictors, that minimises the squared vertical gaps, and how that fit splits the total sum of squares into the part accounted for and the part left over.
  • Show that coding a predictor as 0 or 1 turns the same linear model into an analysis of covariance, a 2×2 ANOVA, or a one-way ANOVA.
  • Explain how squaring a correlation turns it into a share of variance, and why sums of squares add as variances do, with a positive association widening a sum and narrowing a difference.

Association

This path is for how variables move together, from shared movement to the straight line that minimizes squared error, then to the plane that splits the same total into model and error. Coding a predictor as 0 or 1 turns that same linear model into an analysis of covariance, a 2×2 ANOVA, or a one-way ANOVA. The path then turns a correlation into a share of variance. By the end, sum of squares is the same idea as variance, and positive correlation widens a sum and narrows a difference. A short review then asks ten questions on that shared movement. The track ends after the review.

Covariance and Correlation

Shared movement on a common scale

Why

When two variables are measured together, a natural question is whether they tend to move in the same direction. Covariance records that shared movement in the units of both variables; dividing by the two standard deviations turns the record into a correlation on a scale from negative one to one. The size of that correlation also has a meaning in units of variance.

Key points

  • cov(X, Y) = Σ (xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) / (n − 1)shared movement, in the units of both variables.xᵢ and yᵢ are one pair, x̄ and ȳ their means, n how many pairs, and Σ adds the pairs.
  • r = cov(X, Y) / (sx sy)that shared movement on a scale from −1 to 1.sx and sy are the two standard deviations.

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)

The smallest squared gaps

Why

A straight line through paired measurements is a claim about how one variable changes with the other. Ordinary least squares chooses the intercept and the slope that make the squared vertical gaps as small as they can be. That minimum is the error sum of squares. The same line splits the total sum of squares into a part the line accounts for and a part left over. The share the line accounts for is the proportion of variance in the outcome that the straight line explains.

Key points

  • SSE(b₀, b₁) = Σ (yᵢ − b₀ − b₁ xᵢ)²the sum of squared vertical gaps for a chosen line.yᵢ is an outcome, xᵢ its predictor, b₀ the intercept, b₁ the slope, and Σ adds the gaps.
  • β̂₁ = Σ (xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) / Σ (xᵢ − x̄)²the slope that makes those squared gaps as small as they can be.xᵢ and yᵢ are one pair, and x̄ and ȳ are the means.
  • β̂₀ = ȳ − β̂₁ x̄the intercept that goes with that slope.ȳ and x̄ are the means, and β̂₁ is the fitted slope.
  • SST = SSR + SSEthe total sum of squares splits into the part the line accounts for and the part left over.SST is the total, SSR the regression sum of squares, and SSE the error sum of squares.
  • R² = SSR / SSTthe share of the total that the line accounts for.SSR is the regression sum of squares, and SST is the total.

Multiple Regression

From a line to a plane

Why

Ordinary least squares on one predictor draws a line and splits the total sum of squares into the part that line accounts for and the part left over: SST = SSR + SSE. With two predictors the fitted surface is a plane, and the same split is written SST = SSM + SSE. SSM is the sum of squared distances from the fitted values to the mean of Y, and SSE is the sum of squared vertical gaps the plane does not close. R² is the share SSM takes of SST.

Key points

  • Ŷ = b₀ + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂the fitted plane.Ŷ is a fitted value, b₀ the intercept, and b₁ and b₂ the slopes on the predictors X₁ and X₂.
  • SST = Σ (Yᵢ − Ȳ)²the total sum of squared deviations from the mean of Y.Yᵢ is one outcome, Ȳ that mean, and Σ adds them.
  • SSM = Σ (Ŷᵢ − Ȳ)²the part the plane accounts for: squared distances from the fitted values to the mean.Ŷᵢ is a fitted value, and Ȳ is the mean of Y.
  • SSE = Σ (Yᵢ − Ŷᵢ)²the squared vertical gaps the plane does not close.Yᵢ is an outcome, and Ŷᵢ is its fitted value.
  • SST = SSM + SSEthe total is the plane’s share plus the leftover.SST is the total sum of squares, SSM the model sum of squares, and SSE the error sum of squares.
  • R² = SSM / SSTthe share of the total that the plane accounts for.SSM is the model sum of squares, and SST is the total.

Multiple Regression and ANOVA Equivalence

The same line, with a 0 and a 1

Why

A plane fitted to two continuous predictors is still ordinary least squares: Ŷ = b₀ + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂, and a product term b₃X₁X₂ bends that plane when the slope of one predictor depends on the other. Coding one predictor as 0 or 1 turns the same equation into an analysis of covariance: one slope for each group, and b₃ is the difference between those slopes. Coding both predictors as 0 or 1 is a 2×2 ANOVA, and the same product is the interaction. Dummy codes for three groups use two 0/1 predictors and no product term. That fit is a one-way ANOVA: the intercept is the reference mean, and each coefficient is a difference between a group mean and that reference.

Key points

  • Ŷ = b₀ + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂ + b₃X₁X₂a plane, plus a product so one slope can depend on the other predictor.Ŷ is the fitted value, b₀ the intercept, b₁ and b₂ the slopes, and b₃ how much one slope changes with the other predictor.
  • X₂ is 0 or 1, and b₃ is the difference in slopes
  • both predictors are 0 or 1: a 2×2 ANOVA
  • Ŷ = b₀ + b₁D₁ + b₂D₂dummy codes for three groups, the same fit as a one-way ANOVA.D₁ and D₂ are 0 or 1, b₀ is the reference group’s mean, and each other coefficient is a difference from that mean.

Variance Explained

A correlation turned into a share

Why

A correlation says how strongly two variables line up; squaring it turns that strength into a share of variance. That square is the same kind of share the linear model calls R²: the proportion of variance in one variable that a straight-line relationship with the other accounts for.

Key points

  • R² = r²square the correlation and the result is a share of variance.r is the correlation, and R² is that share.
  • R² = SSline / SStotalthe same share: the part a straight line accounts for, over the total.SSline is the line’s sum of squares, and SStotal is the total.

Variance Explained

What’s next?

Squaring a correlation turns shared movement into a share of variance. When two sources share nothing, that share is zero, the covariance drops out, and the variances add — and so do the sums of squares. A positive association puts the covariance back, widening a sum and narrowing a difference.

Additivity of Variance & SS

Adding squares, and the correlation

Why

Sum of squares is simply another way to measure deviation — the same idea as variance, kept as a total rather than an average. Because squared deviations are always positive, those totals add cleanly. The catch is mild: since they are squares, adding or subtracting two variables still has to carry their correlation, which widens a sum and narrows a difference when the association is positive.

Key points

  • Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2 Cov(X, Y)the variance of a sum. A positive covariance widens it.Var is a variance, and Cov is the covariance.
  • Var(X − Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) − 2 Cov(X, Y)the variance of a difference. A positive covariance narrows it.Var is a variance, and Cov is the covariance.
  • SS(X + Y) = SS(X) + SS(Y) + 2 SP(X, Y)the same relation for totals rather than averages.SS is a sum of squares, and SP is the sum of products of the paired deviations.
  • SS(X − Y) = SS(X) + SS(Y) − 2 SP(X, Y)a positive sum of products narrows the difference.SS is a sum of squares, and SP is the sum of products of the paired deviations.
