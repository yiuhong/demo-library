The 5-Level Conceptual Hierarchy of Statistics

A claim built in levels

Why

A statistical claim is built in levels. A parameter is a fixed rule of the process. The process produces one outcome. A random variable turns that outcome into a number. The distribution is the pattern of those numbers. Inference uses the sample to say something about the parameter you cannot see.

Key points

  • parameter θ fixes the process
  • X : Ω → ℝa random variable turns an outcome into a number.X is that number, Ω is the set of outcomes, and ℝ is the real numbers.
  • μ = E[X], σ² = Var(X)the mean and the variance of X.E[X] is the expected value, the long-run average, and Var(X) is the average squared deviation from it.
  • X̄ ± 1.96 · (s / √M)an interval for the mean from one sample.X̄ is the sample mean, s the sample standard deviation, M how many observations, and 1.96 the normal cutoff for about 95%.

Building the Normal Distribution

A curve fixed by center and spread

Why

Many descriptions of data end by asking whether the shape looks like a familiar smooth curve. The normal distribution is one such curve, fixed by a center and a spread. Putting the curve together shows what the model is made of.

Key points

  • f(x) = (1 / (σ√(2π))) exp(−(x − μ)² / (2σ²))the height of the normal curve at x.x is a point on the axis, μ the center, σ the spread, π is pi, and exp is the exponential.
  • μ = center
  • σ = spread

Probability Distributions Map

The normal curve among the others

Why

The normal curve is one model among many. This map places sixteen common distributions in five neighborhoods and draws three kinds of link: a special case that fixes a parameter, a transformation or a sum, and a limit. Binomial(n, p) with n = 1 is Bernoulli(p). As n grows and np settles on λ, the binomial count approaches Poisson(λ). As n grows, the binomial count also approaches a normal curve, the shape the previous step built. That limit is the de Moivre–Laplace theorem: de Moivre (1738), The doctrine of chances (2nd ed.), and Laplace (1812), Théorie analytique des probabilités.

Key points

  • Binomial(1, p) = Bernoulli(p)one trial with success chance p is a Bernoulli trial.p is that chance.
  • Binomial(n, p) → Poisson(λ) as n → ∞ and np → λthe binomial count approaches a Poisson count as the trials grow and the mean np settles on λ.n is how many trials, p the success chance, and λ the Poisson mean.
  • Binomial(n, p) → Normal as n → ∞ (de Moivre–Laplace)with many trials the same count approaches a normal curve.n is how many trials, and p is the success chance.

Binomial Distribution & Bernoulli Process

Counting heads in n tosses

Why

A coin toss is a Bernoulli trial: two outcomes, the same chance of heads every time, and no memory of the tosses before it. The trial and the binomial count are the model in Bernoulli (1713), Ars conjectandi. Count the heads in n tosses and that count is binomial, the sum of those trials. The bars are the exact probabilities.

Key points

  • Xᵢ ~ Bernoulli(p), P(Xᵢ = 1) = pone trial.Xᵢ is 1 for a success and 0 otherwise, and p is the chance of success.
  • X = X₁ + … + Xₙ ~ Binomial(n, p)the count of successes in n trials.Each Xᵢ is one trial, n is how many, and p is the chance of success on each.
  • P(X = k) = C(n, k) · pᵏ · (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏthe chance of exactly k successes.C(n, k) is the number of ways to place those k successes in n trials, and p is the chance of one success.
  • μ = np, σ = √(np(1 − p))the mean and the standard deviation of that count.n is how many trials, and p is the chance of success.

Try this

Set the number of coins and the chance of heads, then run many trials. The observed frequencies settle onto the bars.

Poisson Distribution & Poisson Point Process

Arrivals in a stretch of time

Why

A coin toss fixes the number of trials and counts the successes. A Poisson process fixes a stretch of time and counts arrivals that have no memory of one another. The count distribution is the one in Poisson (1837), Recherches sur la probabilité des jugements en matière criminelle et en matière civile. The waiting time between arrivals is exponential with the same rate, and the count in an interval of length t is Poisson(λt). Chop that interval into many short bins and each bin is nearly a Bernoulli trial. As the bins get finer, the binomial count approaches the Poisson count.

Key points

  • N(t) ~ Poisson(λt)the number of arrivals in a window of length t.λ is the rate, arrivals per unit of time.
  • P(N(t) = k) = e−λt (λt)k / k!the chance of exactly k arrivals.λt is the mean count, e is the exponential base, and k! is k factorial: 1 × 2 × … × k, and 0! = 1.
  • μ = λtthe mean number of arrivals.λ is the rate per unit of time, and t is the length of the window.
  • Binomial(n, μ/n) → Poisson(μ) as n → ∞split the window into n bins and the binomial count approaches the Poisson count.μ is the mean count, and n is how many bins.

Try this

Set the rate and the window and watch the arrivals. The bars are the Poisson probabilities, and a finer binomial count approaches them.

Galton Board

Bounces that pile into a bell

Why

Each bead on a Galton board takes a left or right bounce at every peg, the same Bernoulli trial as a coin toss. The bin it reaches is the count of right bounces, a binomial sum of independent steps. With more rows, those counts pile up into a bell shape, and the binomial distribution approximates a normal distribution. The board is the quincunx in Galton (1889), Natural inheritance.

Key points

  • X = X₁ + … + Xₙ ~ Binomial(n, p)the bin is a count of right bounces.Each Xᵢ is one bounce, n is the number of rows, and p is the chance of a right bounce.
  • μ = np, σ = √(np(1 − p))the mean and the standard deviation of that count of bounces.n is the number of rows, and p is the chance of a right bounce.
  • P(X = k) ≈ (1 / (σ√(2π))) exp(−(k − μ)² / (2σ²))with many rows, the chance of bin k is about this normal height.k is the bin, μ the mean count, σ its spread, π is pi, and exp is the exponential.

Try this

Start the flow or drop a burst, and change the rows. Each bead’s bin is its count of right bounces, and more rows pile those counts into a bell.

Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

Many means, one curve

Why

A single sample mean is one summary of one set of numbers. If you imagine drawing many samples of the same size, those means themselves form a distribution. As each sample gets larger, that distribution of means settles toward a normal curve, whatever shape the raw observations had. The central limit theorem is that statement about means. The spread of those means is the standard error, σ / √n, so a larger sample pulls them in tighter.

Key points

  • X̄ ≈ N(μ, σ²/n) for large nthe distribution of the sample mean is about normal.X̄ is that mean, μ and σ the population mean and standard deviation, n the sample size, and N(mean, variance) a normal curve.
  • SE = σ / √nthe standard error: how widely those means spread.σ is the population standard deviation, and n is the sample size.

Try this

Draw many samples and watch their means settle toward a normal curve. This simulation uses the population standard deviation; with one real sample that spread is estimated by s / √n.

Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

Horowitz (1974) CLT Simulation

Watching sample means accumulate

Why

The central limit theorem is easier to trust when you can watch sample means accumulate. Horowitz's 1974 classroom simulation draws samples of 10 again and again from about 1,000 scores, roughly N(50, 15.8); the collection of sample means takes on a normal shape. The spread of those means is about σ / √10. Each sample also estimates that spread with s / √n, and the average of those estimates settles near it (Horowitz, 1974, pp. 179–182).

Key points

  • X̄ₙ → N(μ, σ²/n) as n growsthe distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal curve.X̄ₙ is the mean of n scores, μ and σ the population mean and standard deviation, and n the sample size.

Try this

Draw samples of 10 with replacement from about 1,000 scores and watch the means pile up. Each sample’s s / √n estimates the spread of those means.

Chi-Square Distribution

Squared normals, added up

Why

Not every sampling story is about a mean. Square independent standard normal draws and add them up; the sum follows a chi-square distribution, the usual sampling model for a variance. The degrees of freedom are how many squares went in: one square spikes at zero, and further squares move the curve to the right. The same page can drop a sum of squared count residuals onto that curve. That mark is still one draw from the sampling distribution, not a test of a model.

Key points

  • χ²ν = Z₁² + … + Zν²a chi-square value is this sum of squared standard normals.ν is how many squares, and each Z is one standard normal.
  • Zᵢ ~ N(0, 1), independenteach draw is a standard normal, and the draws are independent.Zᵢ is one draw, and N(0, 1) has mean 0 and variance 1.
  • ν = number of squared normals

Target Analogy of Confidence Intervals

A percentage for the procedure

Why

A confidence interval is a range computed from a sample. In the target analogy the unknown value stays fixed and each interval is a shot, so the stated percentage describes the procedure over many samples — not the chance that this one interval covers that value.

Key points

  • CI = X̄ ± z* · (σ / √n)an interval around the sample mean.X̄ is that mean, z* the normal cutoff for the stated percentage, σ the population standard deviation, and n the sample size.
  • coverage ≈ 1 − α over repeated samplesthe share of repeated intervals that hit the fixed value.α is the share that miss.

Chi-Square as Squared Distance

Squared distance from a bullseye

Why

Give each of those normals a spread, and the sum of squares is squared distance from a bullseye. A dart from a Gaussian cloud lands on a rail, a board, or in a chamber. Unequal spreads stretch the cloud, and each axis keeps its own weight: a generalized chi-square. Equal spreads make circles, and the squared distance is one variance times a standard chi-square. Divide each axis by its spread and the squared distance is the standard chi-square from the previous step.

Key points

  • Q = σ1² Z1² + … + σν² Zν²squared distance from the bullseye when each axis has its own spread.Q is that distance, σ1² the variance on the first axis, Z1 a standard normal on that axis, and ν how many axes.
  • equal spreads: Q = σ² χ²νwhen every axis has the same spread, the distance is that variance times a standard chi-square.σ² is the shared variance, and ν is how many axes.
  • divide each axis by its spread: Q = Z1² + … + Zν²after scaling each axis by its spread, the distance is a standard chi-square.Each Z is a standard normal, and ν is how many axes.

Try this

Throw a Gaussian dart and read its squared distance from the bullseye as a generalized, scaled, or standard chi-square.

From Binomial Counts to Chi-Square

A count, a z, then a chi-square

Why

A category from a coin or a die is a binomial count. The expected count is np, and the central limit theorem approximates that count with a normal curve. Dividing the error by the binomial standard deviation produces a z score. With two categories the errors are opposites, so the squared z equals Pearson’s chi-square and there is 1 degree of freedom. With more categories the counts are tied, so the degrees of freedom are k − 1, and one squared z is not that category’s Pearson cell: the squared z divides by E(1 − p), and the cell divides by E. The cells add to the chi-square. Cochran’s rule asks that every expected count be at least 5 before those curves are trusted. Pearson’s criterion is Pearson (1900), On the criterion that a given system of deviations from the probable in the case of a correlated system of variables is such that it can be reasonably supposed to have arisen from random sampling. The expected-count check is Cochran (1954), Some methods for strengthening the common χ² tests.

Key points

  • Eᵢ = n pᵢthe count the model expects in category i.n is how many trials, and pᵢ is the model’s chance for that category.
  • Zᵢ = (Oᵢ − Eᵢ) / √(n pᵢ (1 − pᵢ))that count, scaled into a z score.Oᵢ is the observed count, Eᵢ the expected count, n how many trials, and pᵢ the category’s chance.
  • Pearson cell = (Oᵢ − Eᵢ)² / Eᵢthat category’s contribution to the chi-square.Oᵢ is the observed count, and Eᵢ is the expected count.
  • χ² = Σ (Oᵢ − Eᵢ)² / Eᵢ, df = k − 1the cells add to a chi-square, with degrees of freedom one less than the categories.Oᵢ is observed, Eᵢ expected, df degrees of freedom, k how many categories, and Σ adds the cells.
  • k = 2: Z² = χ²with two categories, the squared z equals the chi-square.k is how many categories.
  • k > 2: Z² divides by Eᵢ(1 − pᵢ)with more categories the squared z uses the binomial variance, so it is not the Pearson cell.Eᵢ is the expected count, and pᵢ is the category’s chance.
  • Cochran: each Eᵢ ≥ 5trust the normal and chi-square curves when every expected count is at least 5.Eᵢ is the expected count.

Pearson’s χ² Goodness-of-Fit

Counts tested against a model

Why

Pearson’s goodness-of-fit test compares observed counts with the counts a stated model expects. Each category contributes (O − E)² / E, the Pearson cell from the previous step, and the contributions add to a chi-square with k − 1 degrees of freedom. That statistic is one point on the sampling curve from the first step. Past the critical value, the counts are surprising if the model is true. Cochran’s rule still asks that each expected count be at least 5 before that curve is a fair reference. Pearson’s criterion is Pearson (1900), On the criterion that a given system of deviations from the probable in the case of a correlated system of variables is such that it can be reasonably supposed to have arisen from random sampling. The expected-count check is Cochran (1954), Some methods for strengthening the common χ² tests.

Key points

  • χ² = Σ (Oᵢ − Eᵢ)² / Eᵢobserved counts against the counts a model expects, added up.Oᵢ is the observed count, Eᵢ the expected count, and Σ adds the categories.
  • Eᵢ = n pᵢ under the modelthe count the stated model expects.n is the total count, and pᵢ is the model’s chance for category i.
  • df = k − 1degrees of freedom, one less than the number of categories.k is how many categories.
  • a large χ² falls past the critical valuethe counts are then surprising if the model is true.χ² is the goodness-of-fit statistic.

Bridge

The next step keeps that formula. The same χ² can be read as goodness-of-fit, when the expected counts come from a known model, or as a test of independence, when they come from the sample margins.

χ² Roulette: Goodness-of-Fit vs Independence

A known model, or the sample margins

Why

The same chi-square compares each observed count with an expected count. In a goodness-of-fit reading the expected counts come from probabilities stated before the sample. Nothing is estimated from the data, so a 2×2 table has 3 degrees of freedom. A test of independence builds those expected counts from the sample’s own row and column totals. Estimating the two margins spends 2 degrees of freedom, and the same four cells are judged on 1 degree of freedom. The sum does not change its form. What changes is where each expected count comes from.

Key points

  • χ² = Σ (Oᵢ − Eᵢ)² / Eᵢthe same sum of squared gaps.Oᵢ is the observed count, Eᵢ the expected count, and Σ adds the cells.
  • goodness-of-fit: Eᵢ = n pᵢ, and pᵢ is knownexpected counts from chances stated before the sample.n is the total count, Eᵢ the expected count, and pᵢ that known chance.
  • independence: E = (row total × column total) / nexpected counts built from the sample’s own margins.E is the expected count in a cell, and n is the grand total.
  • 2×2: df = 3 when pᵢ is known, and df = 1 from the marginsfour cells, judged on 3 degrees of freedom when the chances are known and on 1 when the margins supply the expected counts.df is degrees of freedom, and pᵢ is a chance stated in advance.
