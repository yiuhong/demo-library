t-test

z-Distribution vs t-Distribution

Estimated spread, heavier tails

Why

When a mean is compared against a known standard deviation, a z curve is the usual reference. In practice that standard deviation is almost always estimated from the same data, and the extra uncertainty shows up as heavier tails than z. When that estimate of the spread comes out small, the statistic is pushed farther into the tail. Those tails are heavier still in a small sample; that wider curve is the t distribution, and it is the reference you will use for the two comparisons that follow.

Key points

  • z = (X̄ − μ) / (σ / √n)a sample mean compared with a known spread.X̄ is the sample mean, μ the population mean, σ the known standard deviation, and n the sample size.
  • t = (X̄ − μ) / (s / √n)the same comparison when the spread is estimated from the sample, so the tails are heavier.X̄ is the sample mean, μ the comparison value, s the sample standard deviation, and n the sample size.
  • df = n − 1degrees of freedom for this one sample.n is the sample size.

Independent Samples t-Test

Two groups that do not share people

Why

One common comparison is two groups that do not share people — each person is in one group only. An independent-samples t-test asks whether the two group means differ by more than you would expect from the spread within the groups, using a t curve whose standard error combines those two spreads.

Key points

  • t = (X̄₁ − X̄₂) / SEhow far the two means stand apart, in standard errors.X̄₁ and X̄₂ are the group means, and SE is the standard error of their difference.
  • SE = √(sp² (1/n₁ + 1/n₂))that standard error, from one pooled variance.sp² is the pooled variance, and n₁ and n₂ are the two sample sizes.
  • sp² = ((n₁ − 1) s₁² + (n₂ − 1) s₂²) / (n₁ + n₂ − 2)the pooled variance, the two group spreads combined.s₁² and s₂² are the group variances, and n₁ and n₂ are the sample sizes.

Try this

Paired Sample t-Test

One difference per person

Why

In a paired design, the two scores from each person become one difference. That removes person-to-person variation before the mean difference is set against a t curve. The path ends here: the same family of reference curve, and two designs for which scores enter the comparison.

Key points

  • dᵢ = xᵢ,post − xᵢ,preone person’s change, later score minus earlier score.xᵢ,post is the later score, and xᵢ,pre is the earlier one.
  • t = d̄ / (sd / √n), with μ₀ = 0the mean change set against a t curve, for a hypothesized change of zero.d̄ is the mean difference, sd its standard deviation, n how many pairs, and μ₀ that hypothesized mean.
  • df = n − 1degrees of freedom, one less than the number of pairs.n is how many pairs.

Statistical power

This path is for the chance of detecting a real effect, from the definition to how that chance grows. By the end you can say what power is and what a larger effect or sample does to it. A short review then asks ten questions on that chance. The track ends after the review.

Statistical Power

The chance of detecting a real effect

Why

A significance cutoff is set under the null, but a real effect shifts the sampling distribution of the test statistic. Power is the chance of detecting an effect that is really there: the share of that alternative distribution that lands beyond the null cutoff.

Key points

  • power = P(reject H₀ | H₁ true)the chance of detecting an effect that is really there.H₀ is the null, no effect, and H₁ is the alternative, a real effect.
  • α = P(reject H₀ | H₀ true)the chance of a false detection when there is no effect.α is that rate, and H₀ is the null.

Statistical Power

Bridge

Once that definition is clear, the next question is how power changes when the study changes.

Power Curve

Power moves with effect and sample

Why

Power is not a fixed number; it moves with the study. A larger effect or a larger sample moves the alternative distribution farther past the null cutoff, and more of it counts as a detection.

Key points

  • power rises with |effect| and with na larger effect or a larger sample makes a real effect easier to detect.n is the sample size, and |effect| is the size of the effect, ignoring its sign.
  • SE shrinks as 1/√nthe standard error gets smaller as the sample grows, in proportion to one over the square root of n.SE is that standard error, and n is the sample size.

From t-Test to ANOVA

The same question for more means

Why

With two groups, a t-test asks whether the means differ by more than the scatter inside the groups. ANOVA asks the same kind of question when there may be more than two means: is the variation among group means large relative to the variation left inside groups?

Key points

  • F = MSbetween / MSwithinvariation among the means, relative to variation inside the groups.MSbetween and MSwithin are those mean squares: a sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom.
  • for two groups, t² = Fthe squared two-group t equals this F.t is that t statistic, and F is the ratio of the mean squares.

Bridge

When each person is measured more than once, that inside scatter depends on how their scores move together.

From Paired t-Test to RM-ANOVA

Individual differences set aside

Why

The difference-score standard deviation already has the pre–post correlation built into it. A more positive covariance shrinks sD: when scores move together, people who score high at one timepoint tend to score high at the other, so individual change scores stay small — and that smaller sD is what feeds Residual. The same positive covariance enlarges SS Subjects, because subject sums of squares track how far each person's average sits from the grand mean. Together, SS Subjects and Residual make up within-conditions: the scatter around each timepoint mean. By partitioning out those individual differences, the paired t and repeated-measures ANOVA cut noise that an independent test would leave intact. An independent test treats all of within-conditions as residual.

Key points

  • sD² = s₁² + s₂² − 2 r s₁ s₂the variance of the person-by-person differences.s₁ and s₂ are the standard deviations at the two times, and r is their correlation.
  • Within-conditions = Subjects + Residualscatter around the timepoint means splits into individual differences and what is left.
  • SS Residual = (N − 1) sD² / 2the leftover sum of squares after person differences are set aside.N is how many people, and sD² is the variance of the differences.
  • for k = 2, t² = Fwith two timepoints, the squared paired t equals the repeated-measures F.k is how many timepoints, t that paired statistic, and F the ratio of mean squares.

Try this

Slide the pre–post correlation and see a positive covariance shrink the difference-score spread, enlarge SS Subjects, and feed the residual.

Partitioning Sum of Squares in 1-Way, 2-Way, and 3-Way ANOVA

Where the variation comes from

Why

To compare several means, it helps to say where the variation in the data comes from. With one factor the total splits into a part between groups and a part left inside groups. With two or three factors, and with between-subjects, within-subjects, or mixed designs, that total breaks into more bricks: each main effect, each interaction, and an error term that matches the stratum.

Key points

  • SStotal = sum of the atomic bricksthe total sum of squares, every piece of variation added together.SStotal is that total, and a brick is one piece: an effect or its error.
  • between-subjects: one error term tests every effect
  • within-subjects: each repeated factor has its own error stratumthe error term that belongs to that repeated factor.
  • F = MSeffect / MSerror for the matching stratuman effect tested against its own error term.MSeffect and MSerror are mean squares: a sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom.

Bridge

The next step is the 2×2 between-subjects case, two main effects, their interaction, and one error.

Two-Way ANOVA

Two factors, and their combination

Why

Sometimes each observation belongs to a combination of two factors, not one. Two-way ANOVA labels variation with both factors at once: each factor has its own main effect, and the interaction is the pattern that appears only in the combination. The usual reading of these F tests still depends on conditions on the residuals.

Key points

  • SStotal = SSA + SSB + SSAB + SSerrorthe total sum of squares splits into factor A, factor B, their interaction, and error.Each SS is a sum of squares.
  • FA = MSA / MSerror, and likewise for B and ABfactor A’s mean square divided by the error mean square, and the same for B and the interaction.A mean square is a sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom.

Bridge

The next step is the mixed case, where one factor separates people and the other measures the same people twice.

2×2 Mixed ANOVA

One factor for groups, one for time

Why

A 2×2 mixed design has one factor that separates people into groups and one factor that measures the same people twice. The group effect is tested against differences between people. The time effect and the group-by-time interaction are tested against how people vary in their change. The same picture can be read as two simple comparisons: the midpoints, and the pre-to-post changes.

Key points

  • SSbetween = SSgroup + SSerror(between)differences between people split into the group effect and the rest.Each SS is a sum of squares.
  • SSwithin = SStime + SSgroup×time + SSerror(within)change within people splits into time, the group-by-time interaction, and the rest.Each SS is a sum of squares.
  • Fgroup = MSgroup / MSerror(between)the group effect, tested against differences between people.Each MS is a mean square: a sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom.
  • Ftime = MStime / MSerror(within)the time effect, tested against how people vary in their change.Each MS is a mean square: a sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom.

Bridge

The next step draws this design, and the designs before it, as one fitted line or plane.

Geometric ANOVA Regression

A plane, a line, or two cohorts

Why

The splits so far can be read as one linear model: a score, a coded factor, and the part the fit does not explain. A between-subjects design fits one plane across the factor. In the cross-section that plane is one line, and each person’s baseline stays in the residual, so those residual lines are long. A within-subjects design gives each person a line. The baseline is set aside, and the residuals are short. The mixed design nests those lines in two cohorts, and the cohorts need not share a slope.

Key points

  • between-subjects: Z = b₀ + b₁(Factor 1) + eone line for separate groups.Z is the score, b₀ the intercept, b₁ the slope on the coded factor, and e the residual the line does not explain.
  • within-subjects: Z = (b₀ + u₀) + b₁(Factor 1) + ea line for each person, with that person’s baseline set aside.Z is the score, b₀ a shared intercept, u₀ the person’s own baseline, b₁ the slope, and e the residual.
  • mixed: two cohorts of lines, and the slopes need not match

ANOVA Assumptions

Residuals, spread, and independence

Why

The usual reading of an F test wants residuals that are roughly normal, groups with similar spread, and observations that are independent of each other. Those are the conditions for the standard interpretation — a check on whether the splits and F ratios can be read in the ordinary way. Breaking one of them moves the Type I error under the null, the power under the alternative, or both.

Key points

  • residuals ≈ normal
  • group variances similar
  • observations independent
