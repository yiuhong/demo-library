Chapter 1 – Describing data (draft)

Yiu Hong Ko · Volume 1 draft · living draft aligned with demo-library · as of 25 Nov 2026 HKT · status: Draft for YH review

Chapter learning goals

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Explain how mean, median, and mode summarise a distribution, and when each is appropriate.
  • Connect moments, skew, and visual summaries (dot plot, boxplot, histogram, KDE) to the shape of data.
  • Use Q–Q plots and outlier displays to judge how well a sample matches a reference distribution.
  • Explain why matching summary statistics (means, SDs, correlation) can still hide different graphs (Anscombe / Datasaurus).

Why describe data first?

Before you test a hypothesis or build a model, you need a clear picture of the numbers in front of you. Descriptive summaries answer three everyday questions: Where do the values sit? How spread out are they? What shape do they make? Those answers are not busywork. They decide whether a mean is a fair summary, whether a long tail is pulling the story, whether a few extreme scores deserve a second look, and — by the end of the track — why a matching set of summaries can still hide a different graph.

In psychology and methods courses, the temptation is to jump straight to testing and inference. Hold off for a moment. A careful description of the sample is the foundation for honest claims later: if the shape is skewed, if outliers crowd one side, or if the sample looks nothing like the reference distribution you assumed, later analyses will be shaky even if the software runs cleanly.

Center and spread

This first sub-path is about where a set of numbers sits and how wide they are: three kinds of average, moments as averages of spread and skew, and why a sample variance divides by n − 1.

Center and spread

1. Mean, Median, and Mode

Three kinds of average

Live demo: Mean, Median, and Mode

Why

We often describe data by their averages: numerical summaries that answer where the numbers sit. The mean, the median, and the mode are three such answers. They line up when the numbers are roughly balanced, and they pull apart when the shape is not. Learning to watch them move — not only to compute them — is the first step toward reading a distribution rather than a single number.

Light formulae from the track: x̄ = (Σ xᵢ) / n; median = middle value after sorting; mode = most frequent value.

Try this

  1. Open the demo and notice where the three averages sit on the current dot plot.
  2. Drag one or two points toward a long right-hand tail. Watch which average moves farthest.
  3. Pull points back into a tight, roughly symmetric clump. Confirm that mean, median, and mode come close again.
  4. Ask yourself: if you had to report one center for a skewed sample, which average would you defend — and why?

Check your understanding

  1. For a right-skewed sample of reaction times, which tends to sit farthest into the long tail — mean, median, or mode — and why?
  2. Name one research situation where the median might be a fairer “typical” value than the mean.

Bridge

When mean, median, and mode disagree, center alone is not enough. The next question is what other kinds of average can capture spread and shape, not only where the pile sits. That leads to moments.

Center and spread

2. Moments

Averages of spread and skew

Live demo: Moments

Why

There are different types of averages. In statistics, averages of powers of the values (or of their deviations from the mean) are called moments. The mean is the first moment; higher moments give numbers for spread and for skew. Seeing moments as “averages of powers” keeps the idea concrete: you are still summarising the same sample, just asking a richer question of it.

Light formulae: m₁ = x̄; m₂ = Σ (xᵢ − x̄)² / n; m₃ = Σ (xᵢ − x̄)³ / n.

Try this

  1. Start with a small sample and note the first, second, and third moments.
  2. Move one extreme point farther out. Compare how m₂ (spread) and m₃ (skew-related) respond.
  3. Notice that these calculations treat the sample mean as if it were already known — a quiet assumption that matters once you estimate variance from the same data.

Check your understanding

  1. In one sentence, what does the second moment measure that the first moment does not?
  2. Why does it matter that moments built around x̄ treat that mean as “known”?

Bridge

That last point is easy to miss. When the mean is estimated from the sample itself, the usual average of squared deviations is a little too small. Correcting that bias is Bessel’s correction — next.

Center and spread

3. Bessel's Correction

Why divide by n − 1

Live demo: Bessel's Correction

Why

When we use the data to estimate how spread out they are, dividing the sum of squared deviations by (n) tends to undershoot the true variance, because the sample mean was taken from those same values. Dividing by (n - 1) instead corrects for that. The demo lets you see the difference across many simulated samples, not just in a formula.

Light formulae: s² = Σ (xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1); s = √(s²).

Try this

  1. Run the simulation and compare the average variance estimate when dividing by (n) versus (n - 1).
  2. Note where each average sits relative to the true variance line.
  3. Change sample size if the controls allow, and ask whether the gap between the two divisors shrinks as (n) grows.

Check your understanding

  1. Why does dividing by (n) tend to produce a variance estimate that is a little too small?
  2. In your own words, what does Bessel’s correction buy you when you report sample standard deviation?

Bridge

With a fairer number for spread, you can ask a new question about shape: is the distribution pulled one way rather than balanced? That imbalance is skew.

Shape and pictures

4. Skewed Distribution

When a long tail pulls the mean

Live demo: Skewed Distribution

Why

Not every set of numbers is balanced around its center. A long tail on one side pulls the mean toward itself, so mean, median, and mode sit in different places. That imbalance is skew. A single skew number is useful, but the same idea is often clearer when you watch the three averages separate as the shape changes.

Light formulae: skew = (1/n) Σ ((xᵢ − x̄) / σ)³; σ² = Σ (xᵢ − x̄)² / n.

Try this

  1. Drag the skew control from near-symmetric toward a strong right skew. Watch mean, median, and mode separate.
  2. Flip or reverse toward left skew if available, and confirm the mean moves into the long tail again.
  3. Pause on a moderately skewed shape and decide which average you would quote in a methods section — and what caveat you would add.

Check your understanding

  1. In a right-skewed distribution, where does the mean typically sit relative to the median?
  2. Why might reporting only the mean be misleading for a strongly skewed outcome measure?

Bridge

A number for skew is helpful, but the same shape is easier to see in a picture of how values pile up and where the middle of the data sits. Histograms and boxplots are next.

Shape and pictures

5. Boxplot and Histogram

The pile beside the box

Live demo: Boxplot and Histogram

Why

Numbers for center, spread, and skew summarise a shape; pictures show that shape directly. A histogram shows how values pile up across bins. A boxplot packs the center and spread of the same data into a box and whiskers (quartiles and the range of typical values). Reading both together on one sample trains you to match a pile of bars to a five-number story.

Light formulae: Q₁, Q₂ (median), Q₃; IQR = Q₃ − Q₁.

Try this

  1. Look at the histogram first: where is the tallest bar, and is there a visible tail?
  2. Match that picture to the boxplot: locate the median line and the box edges (Q₁, Q₃).
  3. Trace the whiskers and ask how they relate to the outer values in the histogram.
  4. Mentally sketch what would change if a few extreme scores appeared on one side.

Check your understanding

  1. What does the width of the box (the IQR) tell you that the median alone does not?
  2. Give one advantage of the histogram and one advantage of the boxplot for the same sample.

Bridge

Reading both displays together also raises a sharper question: how far can a point sit before it looks unusual against the middle of the data? That is the job of fences and outlier rules.

Shape and pictures

6. Boxplot and Outliers

Points past the fence

Live demo: Boxplot and Outliers

Why

Once you can see the middle of the data in a boxplot, the next question is which points sit so far out that they count as unusual. Tukey fences mark how far a point can sit from the quartiles before it counts as an outlier: typically 1.5 × IQR beyond Q₁ or Q₃. The whiskers stop at the farthest non-outlier; points beyond the fences are flagged.

Light formulae: lower fence = Q₁ − 1.5 × IQR; upper fence = Q₃ + 1.5 × IQR.

Try this

  1. Identify Q₁, the median, Q₃, and the dashed fence lines on the plot.
  2. Add or move an extreme point and watch whether it falls outside the fences.
  3. Notice that a point can sit past the whisker tip and still be inside — or outside — the fence rule, depending on the sample.
  4. Ask: would you drop that point, winsorise it, or keep it and discuss it? (The demo shows the flag; your methods section decides the policy.)

Check your understanding

  1. In words, how is a Tukey fence built from the quartiles and the IQR?
  2. Why is “outside the fence” a useful alert rather than an automatic reason to delete a score?

Bridge

The histogram’s bars still depend on where the bin edges fall. A smooth curve can follow the same pile without those cuts. That smooth picture is the kernel density estimate.

Shape and pictures

7. Kernel Density Estimate (KDE)

A smooth curve, not bin edges

Live demo: Kernel Density Estimate (KDE)

Why

A histogram’s bars move when the bin edges move, even though the numbers have not. A kernel density estimate places a small kernel on each point and adds those kernels, so the same pile becomes a smooth curve. The bandwidth (h) is how wide each kernel is: a narrow kernel keeps the spikes; a wide one flattens them. The same smooth curve underlies violin plots, and a half of it is the cloud in a raincloud plot — so once you can read a KDE, those modern displays feel less mysterious.

Light formulae: f̂(x) = (1/(n h)) Σ K((x − xᵢ) / h); h = bandwidth.

Try this

  1. Compare the histogram and the smooth KDE on the same sample. Where do they agree?
  2. Change the bandwidth: try a narrow h (spiky) and a wide h (over-smoothed). Find a middle setting that still shows the main peak without inventing noise.
  3. If kernel shape options appear (Gaussian, Epanechnikov, boxcar), switch among them and notice that bandwidth usually matters more than the exact kernel for a first look.
  4. Mentally link the curve to a violin or raincloud: same density idea, different packaging beside a box.

Check your understanding

  1. What problem with histograms does a KDE try to soften?
  2. What happens to the density curve if you choose a bandwidth that is much too large?

Bridge

Outliers are one kind of mismatch with a simple shape. Another is whether the whole set of numbers matches a reference distribution — often the normal. Q–Q plots make that comparison visual.

Same summaries, different graphs

8. Q–Q Plot

The sample against a reference curve

Live demo: Q–Q Plot

Why

A few extreme points are one way a set of numbers can look unusual; another is that the overall shape does not match a familiar reference curve. A Q–Q plot lines the sample’s quantiles up against a reference distribution, often the normal. Points near a straight line suggest a good match; a systematic curve away from the line means the sample’s shape and the reference shape differ. It is a last check on whether the description you built fits a familiar model.

Light formula idea: plot (theoretical quantile, sample quantile) for each order statistic.

Try this

  1. Open the demo and compare sample quantiles to the reference line.
  2. Note where points sit in the middle versus the tails — tails often peel away first.
  3. Ask what a banana-shaped departure (or an S-curve) would imply about skew or heavy tails relative to the reference.
  4. Tie it back: if the Q–Q plot looks poor, revisit skew, outliers, and whether a mean-based summary is still your best friend.

Check your understanding

  1. If points in a normal Q–Q plot bend upward in the right tail, what does that suggest about the sample relative to a normal reference?
  2. How does a Q–Q plot complement (not replace) a boxplot-and-outlier display?

Bridge

Matching a reference shape is one check. Another is whether summaries of center, spread, and association can still hide very different graphs. Anscombe’s quartet and the Datasaurus dozen make that limitation vivid — next.

Same summaries, different graphs

9. Limitations of summary statistics

Same summaries, different pictures

Live demo: Limitations of summary statistics

Why

A Q–Q plot can show that a sample’s shape and a reference shape differ. Summaries of center, spread, and correlation can still agree while the pictures do not. Anscombe’s quartet and the Datasaurus dozen keep the means, the standard deviations, and r fixed, and the graph changes from a line to a curve, a stack, a star, or a dinosaur. Switch shapes and those summaries stay put. Drag a point and they move. Re-lock them and the strange picture can return with the same numbers. When the averages match, the graph is still part of the description.

Light formula idea: same x̄, sₓ, ȳ, sᵧ, and r across shapes.

Try this

  1. Open the demo and switch among Anscombe panels or Datasaurus shapes. Confirm the shared summaries while the scatter changes.
  2. Drag a point and watch the means, SDs, and correlation move.
  3. Re-lock the summaries if the controls allow, and see whether a strange picture can return with the same numbers.
  4. Ask: if a paper reported only those summaries, what would a reader miss about the relationship?

Check your understanding

  1. What do Anscombe’s quartet and the Datasaurus dozen hold fixed across panels that the scatter plots do not?
  2. Why is “same means and correlation” not enough to claim two bivariate samples tell the same story?

Bridge

You now have a toolkit for describing a sample — centers, moments and corrected spread, skew, pictures (histogram, boxplot, KDE), outlier fences, a check against a reference shape, and a closing reminder that matching summaries are not the same as matching graphs. Chapter 2 turns from description toward probability and sampling — how samples behave when you draw them again and again.

Chapter wrap

You can now look at a dataset and say more than “the average is…”. You can choose among mean, median, and mode; connect higher moments and Bessel’s correction to a fairer sense of spread; name skew when the three averages separate; and read that shape in a histogram, boxplot, KDE, and Q–Q plot, including Tukey fences for unusual points. You also know why matching means, SDs, and correlation (Anscombe / Datasaurus) are not enough without the graph. That descriptive fluency is what honest inference needs next.

When you are ready, move toward Chapter 2 on probability and sampling: how summaries behave across repeated samples, and why the picture of this sample is only the start of the story.
