3. The Chi-Square Distribution

Exact PDF Empirical Histogram This draw

1. Hard boundary at 0: Squaring any number yields $\ge 0$.

2. Right-skew at low $k$: ~68% of $Z \in [-1, 1]$. When squared, these shrink toward 0 (e.g., $0.5^2 = 0.25$), stacking heavily near 0. Rare outliers explode outward.

3. Central Limit Theorem: As $k$ increases, summing more independent variables pulls the shape toward a normal distribution. Because $\chi^2_k$ is exactly the sum of $k$ independent variables (the $Z_i^2$ terms), rare extreme high values are increasingly likely to be balanced out by the much more common values near zero as $k$ grows, guaranteeing their overall sum clusters symmetrically into a bell curve.

1. A squared residual. $(O_i - E_i)/\sqrt{E_i}$ measures a count in standard deviations from its expected value. Squaring those pieces and adding them is the same kind of sum as $\sum Z_i^2$.

2. One less degree of freedom. The observed counts add to $n$, and the expected counts add to $n$, so the pieces are tied together. A coin has 2 counts and $df = 1$. A die has 6 faces and $df = 5$.

3. The curve is the sampling distribution. One draw places a single statistic on the curve. Many draws fill the histogram. The match is closer when each expected count is not tiny.

Pearson’s χ² Goodness-of-Fit runs one test on a roulette wheel. This case stays with the sampling distribution of the count statistic.
