Partitioning Sum of Squares in ANOVA

Discover why total variation (Sum of Squares) breaks down cleanly into Group Effect and Individual Deviation, and see why the cross-product term magically vanishes!

1 The Group Project Analogy

Imagine students taking part in group projects. A student’s final score depends on two things: how well their overall team performed, and how much higher or lower that student scored relative to their team's average.

Total Score Deviation
Student Score − Class Mean

How far an individual student's score is from the overall class grand average.

Group Component $(a)$
Group Mean − Class Mean

How much the team's environment raised or lowered all team members together.

Individual Component $(b)$
Student Score − Group Mean

The student's unique personal contribution above or below their own team average.

1. Partitioning of Total Deviation
$\underbrace{(y_{ij} - \bar{y})}_{\text{Total Deviation}} = \underbrace{(\bar{y}_i - \bar{y})}_{\text{Group Effect } (a)} + \underbrace{(y_{ij} - \bar{y}_i)}_{\text{Individual Component } (b)}$

Notice that if you simplify the right side, $\bar{y}_i$ cancels out, proving the equality always holds true!

2. Partitioning of Sum of Squares (Squaring & Summing)
$SS_{\text{Total}} = SS_{\text{Between}} + SS_{\text{Within}}$
$\underbrace{\sum_{i}\sum_{j} (y_{ij} - \bar{y})^2}_{SS_{\text{Total}}} = \underbrace{\sum_{i} n_i (\bar{y}_i - \bar{y})^2}_{SS_{\text{Between}}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i}\sum_{j} (y_{ij} - \bar{y}_i)^2}_{SS_{\text{Within}}}$

Here, $i$ represents the group index, $j$ represents the individual student within that group, and $n_i$ is the number of students in group $i$.

Why do we need to SQUARE the deviations?

1. The "Zero-Sum" Problem

If we try to measure total "spread" by just adding up these raw deviations for everyone, the students above average cancel out the students below average. The sum is mathematically forced to be zero: $\sum (y_{ij} - \bar{y}) = 0$. A sum of 0 tells us nothing about how spread out the class actually is!

2. Squaring solves it

By squaring the deviations $(y_{ij} - \bar{y})^2$, we force every distance to be positive so they accumulate into a "Sum of Squares". Additionally, unlike absolute values, squaring interacts with the math in a way that makes overlapping group-and-individual pieces disappear entirely (which you'll see in Section 3).

3 The Math Magic: Why Cross-Products Sum to Zero

When we square a binomial score decomposition $({a} + {b})^2 = {a}^2 + {b}^2 + 2{a}{b}$, why doesn't the $2{a}{b}$ term corrupt our sum? Let's calculate ${a}$ (Group Effect), ${b}$ (Individual Component), ${a}^2$, ${b}^2$, and $2{a}{b}$ for every single student using the live means below.

0

The Within-Group Sum Rule: $\sum b_{ij} = 0$

By definition, individual scores within any group sum up to that group's mean! Therefore, individual deviations $b_{ij} = (y_{ij} - \bar{y}_i)$ inside any team MUST sum to exactly ZERO. Since group effect $a_i$ is constant for all members of team $i$, the sum of cross-products is: $\sum_{j} 2 a_i b_{ij} = 2 a_i \sum_{j} b_{ij} = 2 a_i \times (0) = 0$
