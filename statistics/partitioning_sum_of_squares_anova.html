Subscript & Operator Notation Guide:
/ Nesting (“nested within”): e.g., S/A, S/AB, or S/ABC denotes different Subjects nested inside Between-Subjects groups/cells. × Crossed Interaction: e.g., A×B×C or B×C×S/A denotes crossed factorial or subject-by-within-factor interaction strata.
🧱 Rules 1 & 2 — Within vs. Between Pools (View 3):
• Rule 1 (SSWithin-Cells Pool): ALWAYS contains individual Subject (S) or nested Subject (S/A, S/AB) variance (Kirk, 2012; Winer, 1971).
• Rule 2 (SSBetween-Cells Pool): ALWAYS contains ONLY experimental factors (A, B, A×B) and NEVER contains S (Keppel & Wickens, 2004).
🧱 Rules 3 & 4 — Error Matching & df Multiplier:
• Rule 3 (Subscript Matching): The error term testing an effect must contain the exact factor letters of that treatment brick (across repeated measures) plus S or S/A (Cornfield & Tukey, 1956; Montgomery, 2012).
• Rule 4 (df Multiplier): Multiply the df of factors outside the nesting slash by the total levels inside: e.g., dfB×S/A = a(b−1)(n−1) (Oehlert, 2010).
🧱 Rule 5 — Design Boundary (Pure vs. Mixed):
• Pure Between: Undivided Within-Conditions error block (SSS/A, SSS/AB, or SSS/ABC).
• Pure Within: Error pool shatters into individualized interactions (SSA×S, SSB×S, SSA×B×S) (Kutner et al., 2005).
🧱 Rule 1 The “Subject” Error Pool

The Within-Conditions Rule (SSWithin-Cells)

The Within-Conditions (SSWithin-Cells) pool ALWAYS contains individual Subject (S) variance or nested Subject variance (S/A, S/AB).

Conceptual Logic: When treatment conditions are held completely constant inside a specific cell, the experimental factors cannot cause variation. Therefore, any remaining variance among scores inside that cell must stem from individual baseline differences among the subjects. In a mixed design, this manifests as SSS/A (subjects nested in groups) and SSB×S/A (how those nested subjects vary across repeated measures).

Academic References:
• Kirk, R. E. (2012). Experimental Design: Procedures for the Behavioral Sciences (4th ed.). Sage. (Partitions total variance into between-blocks and within-blocks variance, isolating random subject effects from fixed treatment boundaries).
• Winer, B. J. (1971). Statistical Principles in Experimental Design (2nd ed.). McGraw-Hill. (Documents that variations within identical treatment combinations are functionally identical to subject-level residual error).
🧱 Rule 2 The “Treatment” Pool

The Between-Conditions Rule (SSBetween-Cells)

The Between-Conditions (SSBetween-Cells) pool ALWAYS contains ONLY the experimental factors (A, B, A×B) and never contains Subject (S) terms.

Conceptual Logic: Between-cell variance evaluates differences between the aggregate averages of the experimental cells. Because calculating a cell mean averages out the unique fluctuations of individual subjects, the subject-level error is effectively washed away. The only systematic forces remaining to cause those cell averages to differ are the independent variables themselves.

Academic References:
• Keppel, G., & Wickens, T. D. (2004). Design and Analysis: A Researcher’s Handbook (4th ed.). Pearson. (Illustrates via a “nested tree” visual paradigm that treatment effects rely strictly on collapsing individual scores into group averages, stripping random subject noise from the fixed factor pool).
🧱 Rule 3 Error Term Selection

The Subscript Matching Rule (F-Ratio Denominators)

The correct error term for testing a treatment effect must contain the exact repeated-factor letters of that treatment brick in its subscript, plus the Subject designation (S or S/A).

Conceptual Logic: This rule dictates how F-ratios are built. To determine if an effect is statistically significant, it must be evaluated against the specific background noise it creates. A within-subjects factor B must be tested against how subjects uniquely react to factor B, making its natural denominator MSB×S/A. A between-subjects factor A does not vary across repeated measures, so it is tested against baseline subject differences within those groups (MSS/A).

Academic References:
• Montgomery, D. C. (2012). Design and Analysis of Experiments (8th ed.). Wiley. (Ch. 12: Formal Expected Mean Squares [EMS] algorithm matching fixed effect subscripts to random interaction subscripts).
• Cornfield, J., & Tukey, J. W. (1956). Average values of mean squares in factorials. JASA, 51(274), 307–369. (Seminal Cornfield-Tukey Algorithm using subscripts to identify exact error terms mechanically).
🧱 Rule 4 Algebraic Bracket Size

The Degrees of Freedom (df) Multiplier Rule

To calculate the degrees of freedom for a nested or interaction error brick, multiply the df of the factor outside the nesting slash by the total levels of the factors inside.

Conceptual Logic: The visual notation of the brick reflects its mathematical size. For subjects nested within a group (S/A), the individual subject df is (n − 1). Because factor A is inside the nesting notation, you multiply by its total levels (a), resulting in a(n − 1). For the interaction error term B×S/A, you multiply the df of B, which is (b − 1), by the nested subject term a(n − 1), yielding a(b − 1)(n − 1).

Academic References:
• Oehlert, G. W. (2010). A First Course in Design and Analysis of Experiments. W. H. Freeman. (Ch. 11 details the visual and arithmetic mechanics of tracking df by multiplying structural brackets based on whether indices are nested or crossed).
🧱 Rule 5 Pure vs. Mixed Fracture

The Design Boundary Rule (Pure vs. Mixed)

The composition of the Within-Conditions pool changes predictably based on whether subjects are nested (Between-Subjects) or crossed (Within-Subjects) across factors.

Conceptual Logic:
• In a Pure Between-Subjects design, subjects provide one data point, leaving the Within-Conditions pool as a single, undivided block (SSS/A, SSS/AB, or SSError).
• In a Pure Within-Subjects design, subjects cross every condition, shattering the single error pool into individualized interaction terms for every factor combination (SSA×S, SSB×S, SSA×B×S).

Academic References:
• Kutner, M. H., Nachtsheim, C. J., Neter, J., & Li, W. (2005). Applied Linear Statistical Models (5th ed.). McGraw-Hill. (Demonstrates how shifting a factor from a between-subjects grouping variable to a repeated measure forces the error pool to fracture from a global residual into distinct factor-by-subject interactions).
