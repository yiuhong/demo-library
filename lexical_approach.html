Step 1: The English Dictionary (1936)

Imagine you are psychologists Gordon Allport and Henry Odbert in 1936. You decide to search through the entire unabridged English dictionary (over 400,000 words) to find every single term that could describe a person's behavior or characteristics. They found about 18,000 words.

Step 3: Finding Patterns

4,500 words is still too many. Raymond Cattell realized many words mean roughly the same thing (synonyms like 'sociable', 'friendly'). He used a statistical technique called Factor Analysis to group words that co-occur together into fundamental dimensions.

Calculate correlations between variables (words): Tip: Closer words and thicker lines = Stronger Correlation

Step 4: The Big Five Model (OCEAN)

While Cattell pioneered the statistical approach, his 16-factor model proved difficult to replicate. Decades later, researchers like Paul Costa and Robert McCrae, alongside Lewis Goldberg, refined this work. They repeatedly found that personality traits universally boil down to five broad, robust dimensions. The Lexical Hypothesis ultimately paved the way for this definitive framework: The Big Five.

The Lexical Legacy

By assuming that important traits are encoded in language, psychologists distilled thousands of dictionary words down to five universal dimensions that describe human personality across cultures.
